Subject: Chemistry
Exam style: NSEC (chemical equilibrium, degree of dissociation from vapour density)
Concept tested: Dissociation increases the number of moles, so vapour density falls; α = (D − d) / d for a 1 → 2 dissociation, using vapour density (= M/2), not molar mass
Hook line (top of slide, <=8 words): Lighter vapour, but how much split?
Question: PCl₅(g) ⇌ PCl₃(g) + Cl₂(g). Pure PCl₅ (molar mass 208.5 g mol⁻¹) is heated in a closed vessel. At equilibrium, the vapour density of the gas mixture is 62.55. What fraction of PCl₅ has dissociated?
Options (A-D, or "integer answer"):
A) 0.33
B) 0.40
C) 0.60
D) 0.67
Answer: D) 0.67 (that is, 2/3)
Solution (3-6 short lines, for next day's post/caption):
Vapour density of pure PCl₅: D = 208.5 / 2 = 104.25.
Start with 1 mol PCl₅; at equilibrium total moles = (1 − α) + α + α = 1 + α.
Mass is unchanged, so D/d = 1 + α.
α = (D − d) / d = (104.25 − 62.55) / 62.55 = 41.7 / 62.55 = 2/3 ≈ 0.67.
Why it's tricky (common trap): Students write α = (D − d)/D = 1 − d/D and get 0.40 (option B), dividing by the wrong density. Others plug in molar mass 208.5 instead of vapour density and get an impossible α > 1, then guess. The moles rise to 1 + α, so you divide by the mixture's density.
